## Research implications for selection

Recent forecast-verification research formalizes proper scores built from transformations and aggregation, with complementary diagnostics aimed at the quantities an application cares about. Propriety alone does not make a score informative about every forecast defect. [Pic, Dombry, Naveau and Taillardat, 2025](https://ascmo.copernicus.org/articles/11/23/2025/).

Our application of that principle is already partly implemented: fixed lineup totals and a fixed book's maximum are transformations of the shared player-score vector. Comparing both laws on the **same** roster population can expose failures invisible to player marginals. It does not identify the entire joint distribution or prove a better selection rule. The accepted prospective reader already provides these descriptive comparisons; this is not a proposal to launch a duplicate study.

Transformed kernel scoring research also provides ways to emphasize compound high-impact events while retaining proper scoring. [Allen, Ginsbourger and Ziegel](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12732). A possible later application is a prespecified high-score diagnostic paired with ordinary forecast scores, with thresholds and weights fixed before outcomes. This is a diagnostic option, not evidence for another tail-weight treatment. The project has already tried co-exceedance/variogram instruments and dependence interventions; their failures must remain part of the comparison record.

For every future law change, keep the same chain of evidence: new available information → changed served marginal/joint forecasts → candidate supply → selected set → delivered prefixes → settled performance across slates. Improvement at one stage cannot be assumed to survive the next. Slates and game clusters supply outcome information; thousands of overlapping lineups do not supply thousands of independent NFL tests.
